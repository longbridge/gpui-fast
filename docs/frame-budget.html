A 120 Hz display gives a frame 8.33 ms, and the wall clock never reports less than that, so builds are compared on the CPU time the main thread spends on each frame — where gpui builds, lays out and paints. Extracted gpui used nearly all of it: the grid ran at the edge of the refresh whether it was still or changing. Now the still grid takes less than half the frame, and one where every cell changes two thirds of it.

The gain tracks how much actually changed

The horizontal axis is the share of cells whose text differs from one frame to the next. The baseline is a flat line: it costs the same whether anything changed or not, because it rebuilt every layout node and reordered every primitive either way. The current tree follows the real workload — the quieter the interface, the cheaper the frame — and even with everything changing it is a third cheaper, mostly because ordering what it paints no longer costs what it did.

Where the gain comes from

A frame walks the element tree three times: build constructs it and asks for layout, prepaint resolves positions (layout happens here), paint emits the primitives handed to the GPU. These three are timed inside the frame with Instant, identically in every build measured here, so the compositor cannot distort them.

Prepaint falls from 2.87 ms to 0.57 ms at rest, and paint from 3.32 ms to 0.99 ms at rest and from 3.34 ms to 1.67 ms with every cell changing. In a still frame, layout costs close to nothing: retained nodes are left alone. Paint did not move for a long time, because what it spent was ordering: every primitive is placed in a tree that finds what it overlaps, and that tree had grown thirteen levels deep where five would do. Kept balanced, it costs far less whether or not anything changed, and when nothing moved it is not consulted at all: the orderings a frame asks for are the ones the last frame was given. The phases add up to a little less than the main-thread time above; the rest is presenting the frame.

Build at rest is higher now than as extracted, 1.60 ms against 1.36 ms, though nothing in it got slower: the frame got light enough for the processor to clock down, and the same work takes longer at a lower clock. Checked at the step that hands out last frame's orderings, with another core kept busy, which holds the clock up, build took 1.03 ms and the whole still frame 2.30 ms, against 5.05 ms for the tree before the bounds tree was kept balanced, under the same conditions. Every figure here is at whatever clock the processor chose, so the lightest frames understate what was saved.

Scrolling a wide table

Four lists side by side, each row 24 text cells wide, 109 rows in view and 6000 layout nodes, scrolled by a fixed amount every frame. Rows are keyed by index or not keyed at all, because what applications write is mostly the second. Every row has text of its own, so a row scrolling back into view cannot borrow a neighbour's shaping.

Retaining layout made unkeyed scrolling three times slower before it made it faster. "Before these steps" is the tree this report was first written about. A scrolled list of rows without an id cost 23.43 ms a frame there, against 7.89 ms as extracted, and 5% of frames took about 45 ms — a slowly dragged list stuttered every time it crossed a row. Retained text nodes had stopped asking the line cache for their lines, so the cache forgot them, and every row that moved into a neighbour's node shaped its text again. The steps below that fix it are the reason this table exists.

Scroll columns are the wide table going back and forth at 12 px/frame, unkeyed and keyed by index, and scrolling one way at 48 px/frame unkeyed.

Two steps carry most of the grid: keeping layout nodes (−31% at rest) and keeping the bounds tree balanced (−16% at rest, −22% with everything changing). Two carry most of the scrolling: handing a retained node's lines back to the cache (−65% unkeyed) and matching unidentified list items by their index (−36% more). The measurement closure, the style fingerprint and releasing what removed nodes held each take another 2–11% off whichever workload they touch, and taking a line wrapper only for text that truncates another 7% off a grid whose every cell changes. Keeping a native font per size takes 26% off scrolling at 48 px/frame and 5% off scrolling back and forth: those are the workloads that bring new text into view every frame, and shaping it no longer starts by rebuilding the caches CoreText keeps on a font.

The seven steps after that take another 2–4% off every workload together. Keeping an element's listener lists and accessibility out of line does most of it, 2–4%: a div is 752 bytes instead of 1360, and it is copied every time it moves through a call of its builder. Hashing an element's id path once, giving an element the global id it had last frame and replaying orderings past the bounds that changed each move these benchmarks by 1% or less: their cells have no ids, and few of their orderings change, and the data table below is where those steps pay. Timing layout only once the stats are reset shows up as 2.5% and 4% slower on the grid, and working out a glyph run's rendering once as 3.5% and 5% faster right after it. Under samply, which holds the clock up, the timing step is 3% faster on the grid instead, and the functions whose cost moved between those two builds are the ones the compiler inlined differently, so the two steps are best read together, as 1% faster. Adding memo, which nothing here uses, costs 1% or less.

Handing out last frame's orderings takes 10% off the grid at rest, and only there. It pays off for a frame whose every primitive lands exactly where it did the frame before — one where only colours, opacities or hover states changed. A frame where anything moves or changes size, even one label growing by a digit's width, used to stop matching at that primitive and order the rest as before. Replaying now goes on past the bounds that changed, and orders afresh only what meets them; these grids move by 1% or less for it, and the data table below, whose cells take new values in scattered rows, no longer builds the tree at all.

A figure with a dotted underline comes from runs that disagreed by more than 6%: the machine switched speed while that build was being measured, and its median, and the changes on either side of it, can be off by that much. Hover it for the range of its five runs.

Not everything went one way. Keeping layout nodes costs about 2% when every cell changes: a retained node pays for being found again — its key hashed, its style and children compared, its text keyed — and when nothing can be reused, that is all it gets. The build phase shows more than that, 0.26 ms, but most of it only moved there: extracted gpui dropped every measurement closure and text layout when it cleared the whole tree at the end of a frame, where no phase timer sees it, and a retained node drops its old ones as it is given new ones. A 10% loss for recolouring without reshaping, seen at 25% changing in the first round of measurements, did not survive seven more rounds of each build, which both put at 6.63 ms: that was a slow stretch of the machine, not the change. "Handing lines back" was later replaced by keeping lines cached while something holds them, which does the same job at a fraction of the cost.

In an application: gpui-kit's data table

The benchmarks above are labels and rows without an id. gpui-kit's DataTable story is what an application looks like instead: a 5000-row table of quotes whose cells carry ids, hover states and click handlers, with about a quarter of its first 2000 rows given new values 30 times a second. Most of its elements are identified, and every one of them looks its state up by its path of ids several times a frame, which is work none of the steps above touch.

Figures are milliseconds of main-thread CPU per second, in Window::draw and on the main thread as a whole, recorded with samply at 4 kHz: two runs of 20 s for each build, all in one session, release builds of the same gpui-kit code built against each. Under each figure are the change against the row above and, marked total, against gpui-pre, the crates.io snapshot gpui-kit used before it moved to this tree. The window draws about 29 frames a second, set by the refresh timer. samply's sampling keeps the processor clocked up, so these are lower than what a system monitor shows for the same window, where a lightly loaded processor clocks down and the same work takes longer.

Two of these steps are upstream gpui's, from the snapshot gpui-pre was cut from and newer than the one this tree was extracted from. Building an inspector id copied the whole element id stack and hashed it into a map, and looking up inspector state cloned every div's base style, for every element in every frame, with the inspector closed. Both are compiled in only for debug builds or with the inspector feature, so neither reached the benchmarks above; gpui-kit's workspace turns the feature on for its release builds too.

The steps that follow are this table's. Hashing an id's path once, 8%: element state was looked up by it in request layout, prepaint and paint, each lookup hashing a path of names byte by byte to take the state out of last frame's map, again to put it in this frame's, and once more when the frame was finished. Giving an element the global id it had last frame, 3%: it had been copying the whole element id stack into a new id, and dropping it id by id afterwards. Replaying orderings past the bounds that changed, 3%: a quarter of the rows change, scattered, and replaying had stopped at the first. Together with the rest, the table's frame costs 37% less than with gpui-pre.

When the table memoizes its rows

memo(id, key, build) draws a subtree again from what it drew last frame while its key is unchanged, without building, laying out, prepainting or painting it. Given a key for each row by its delegate — gpui-kit's TableDelegate::row_key, which the story answers with a stock's id and a version bumped whenever the stock changes — the table memoizes the row by it and by its own state for the row. That change is gpui-kit's, and not merged; the figures are from a trial of it.

Three rows in four are reused, and the rows cost half what they did. In a profile of the trial, the list of rows costs 15.0 ms/s instead of 29.9: 11.8 of it rebuilding the rows whose stock changed, 1.7 drawing the others from what they drew, and 1.3 deciding which is which. It is half and not a quarter because the rows that are rebuilt are the ones whose text is new: their numbers have to be shaped, their measurements run and their layout computed again, and their primitives no longer land where they did. The rows that did not change were cheap already — their layout nodes, shaped lines and orderings are kept by the steps above — so memoizing them saves less than their number suggests. The rest of the frame, the gallery around the table, its header and its scrollbars, is drawn as before.

The process figures are from ps over 20 s, not samply, and include every thread; they are what the machine spends at the clock it chooses.

Produced by cargo run -p gpui --example grid_frames --release -- 50 50 <percent> and cargo run -p gpui --example scroll_frames --release -- uniform <motion> <speed> <keying> 10000 0 20 4. Every build was compiled from its own commit with the same two examples and the same phase timer added to Window::draw_roots, then run alternately, rotating the order each round; each figure is the median of five runs.
